Featured insight

The founder is often the fastest route—and the biggest bottleneck.

Founder dependency rarely begins as a design flaw. It begins because asking the founder is quicker. The problem appears when “quick” becomes the default operating system.

Founder Dependency

When decisions, customer context and exceptions live in one person’s head, growth adds pressure faster than it adds capacity.

01
Read in 3 minutes

Stop solving every problem. Start making the decision repeatable.

If the team brings the same type of question to you every week, it is no longer an isolated decision. It is a missing rule, unclear boundary or absent review rhythm. The goal is not to remove the founder from every decision; it is to reserve founder attention for decisions that genuinely need founder judgment.

Founder ActionFor the next five working days, note every repeated question that reaches you. Pick the most frequent one and write a simple rule: who can decide, within what limit, and when it must be escalated.

Small operating moves. Visible business change.

Founder Dependency

Turn repeated questions into decision rules.

Capture the question, the information used to answer it and the risk of a wrong call. Give the closest capable role a boundary they can operate within, then review the rule after two weeks.

Founder ActionCreate one decision rule using this sentence: “You can decide ___ up to ___; involve me only when ___.”
People & Teams

Define ownership by outcome, not activity.

“Follow up with leads” sounds clear but produces different interpretations. “Every qualified lead has a dated next step by Friday” gives the owner and founder the same finish line.

Founder ActionRewrite one recurring responsibility as a visible outcome with an owner and review date.
Process & Operations

Fix the handoff where work slows down.

Most process friction sits between roles: sales to delivery, purchase to accounts, or manager to team. Start at the point where information is lost, work returns for correction or nobody knows who moves next.

Founder ActionChoose one delayed handoff. Agree the required input, receiving owner and expected turnaround time.
Sales & Growth

Review movement, not just the final number.

Revenue is a lagging result. A useful weekly review focuses on qualified opportunities, next actions, stalled deals and the reason each important deal will move—or will not.

Founder ActionFor the top ten opportunities, require one owner, one dated next action and one honest obstacle.
Finance & Decision-Making

Put the decision next to the number.

A metric is useful only when it helps someone choose an action. Keep the first dashboard small: cash position, collections due, gross margin signal, sales movement and one operating risk.

Founder ActionAsk of every number in your weekly review: “What decision changes if this moves up or down?” Remove metrics with no answer.
Founder Dependency

Delegation fails when escalation stays vague.

Teams either escalate everything or hide issues too long when the trigger is unclear. A useful escalation rule defines the risk, time or money threshold that brings the founder back in.

Founder ActionSet one red-line trigger for a delegated area: customer risk, cash exposure, deadline slippage or reputational impact.
Execution

End meetings with a visible commitment.

Discussion creates alignment; commitments create movement. Every priority should leave the room with one owner, one next deliverable and one review date that is already on the calendar.

Founder ActionEnd your next review by reading back only three things: owner, deliverable and date.
